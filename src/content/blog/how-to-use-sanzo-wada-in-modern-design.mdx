Most color tools answer a question no designer actually asks. *"Give me an
infinite number of random palettes."* The problem with infinite choice is
the decision you still have to make afterwards — which one? Against what?
For whom?

Sanzo Wada's 1933 *Dictionary of Color Combinations* solves the decision
differently. Instead of producing variety, it produces **constraint** —
348 numbered combinations, each one defensible, each one paired with a
cultural context. Ninety years later, that constraint is still the
fastest way to arrive at a palette you can commit to.

This post is a practical guide to using the archive for real work.

## What Wada actually did

Sanzo Wada (和田 三造, 1883–1967) was a painter, costume designer, and
color researcher. He studied Western oil painting in Tokyo, lived in
Europe, then spent decades back in Japan documenting the colors of
traditional textiles, dyes, woodblock prints, and theatrical costume.

The *Dictionary of Color Combinations* ( <span lang="ja">配色事典</span> )
is what fell out of that work. Six volumes, 348 numbered plates, two or
three colors per plate. Some plates feel like a specific textile —
persimmon and pale indigo, the exact temperature of a Meiji-era shop
curtain. Others feel like a season — the bruised pinks and olive greens
of late autumn. Nothing in the book is random.

The book was a *reference*, not a coffee-table object. You were supposed
to look something up in it.

## Why constraints beat generators

Palette generators (Coolors, Khroma, Paletton, and every "AI color" tool
released in the past two years) all produce the same shape of output: an
effectively unbounded stream of novel combinations. The designer's job is
then to find the one that works.

This is the wrong division of labor. Finding is harder than generating.
The generator is doing the easy half and handing you the hard half.

Wada's 348 combinations reverse the split. Every one of them is curated.
Every one has been used, on real materials, by real people, in a specific
context. Your job shrinks from *invent something defensible* to *find the
one that fits*.

When the space is small and every item in it is good, your decision
becomes a matter of fit, not judgment. That is a much faster loop.

## Three modes of use

There are three distinct ways to use a curated dictionary of color
combinations in modern design. They correspond to three different phases
of a project.

### 1. Reference mode — match, then borrow

Use this at the start of a brief, when the client has a mood but not a
palette. The mood is usually a *reference* — "we want it to feel like a
Kyoto tea house", "like an autumn hike in Vermont", "like a 1970s fashion
editorial".

Scan the archive for a plate that already evokes that mood. Use those
colors, unmodified, as the starting point for your system.

The archive is organized with this in mind. [Browse by hue family](/colors)
when the mood is color-led ("I need something deep red"). [Browse by
collection](/collections) when the mood is usage-led ("I need something
that reads as branding" or "something autumnal"). [Browse the full
catalog](/browse) with era and mood filters when the mood is cultural
("something Heian", "something Edo-merchant").

Once you've picked a plate, you have your two or three anchor colors.
Your system-level work — building a neutral ramp, an accent, a status set
— follows from there.

### 2. Bounded exploration mode — shift, don't invent

Use this when a client has a brand color they can't move off of, and you
need to find the rest of the system without a generator.

Pick your fixed color. Open the archive and find three to five plates
that include a color near yours on the color wheel. The *other* colors
in those plates are your exploration space.

This is different from what a generator does. A generator gives you
mathematical derivatives of your fixed color — the triadic, the
analogous, the complementary. The dictionary gives you *colors that
historical practice paired with this color*. Those two sets only overlap
by accident.

The practical shortcut: the [color dictionary](/colors) has a detail page
for every named color in the archive. Each page shows every palette that
contains that color. Start from your brand color, click the closest
dictionary color, scroll the palettes, pick the plate whose secondary
feels right.

### 3. Vocabulary mode — learn the names

Use this when you want to *talk* about color more precisely than "warm
beige".

The traditional Japanese color names in the archive are much more
specific than their English counterparts. There is no single word for
"red" in the Japanese shikisai tradition: there is <span lang="ja">紅</span>
(*kurenai*, safflower crimson), <span lang="ja">朱</span> (*shu*,
vermillion), <span lang="ja">茜</span> (*akane*, madder root), <span
lang="ja">丹</span> (*ni*, cinnabar), <span lang="ja">緋</span> (*hi*,
scarlet), and about a dozen others, each anchored to a specific pigment
or dye and usually to a specific social context.

Learning even twenty of these names buys you precision. "The accent
should lean *kurenai* rather than *shu*" is a sentence a print designer
will understand. "The accent should be more crimson-like than
vermillion-like" is the same sentence in English and carries the same
information. Reach for whichever vocabulary is cleaner for your team.

## A worked example

A friend needed a palette for a small indie publisher's website — books
about Japanese literature in translation. She'd already committed to
using <span lang="ja">群青</span> *gunjō* ultramarine as the brand
color, which is `#1B3E90`-ish — a deep saturated blue associated with
late-Edo prints.

In generator mode, she would have ended up with one of the standard
blue-centered systems: a lighter tint, a darker shade, a warm accent,
a neutral. Fine, but generic.

In dictionary mode, she searched the archive for combinations that used
a blue near *gunjō*. Three plates came up immediately: a Heian-court
blue-and-crimson, a Meiji workshop blue-and-ochre, and a 1930s blue-and-
cream. None of these are what a triadic color picker would have
produced.

She picked the Meiji plate: the deep ultramarine against an aged,
mustard-ish ochre. That ochre became her secondary. A warm neutral
(approaching *kinari*, raw silk) became the background. A muted
safflower became the accent. The total system was four colors, derived
in about twenty minutes, defensible in a client review because every
color had a place of origin.

That's the dictionary doing work a generator can't.

## What to ignore

Two things in the archive do not translate directly to modern screen
design, and you should be honest about them.

**The exact hex values.** The archive's hex values are reconstructions.
The original plates were printed in 1933 using pigments that have no
exact RGB equivalent, and the reconstructions come from a community
project that made sensible calls but not authoritative ones. Use the hex
values as a starting point; expect to nudge them by 5–10 points for
legibility or contrast reasons. That's a feature, not a bug — Wada
himself would have adjusted the palette to the printer, the paper, the
ink run.

**The cultural context where it's cosplay, not context.** A *Heian* tag
on a palette tells you something about the historical provenance. It
does not tell you that the palette is appropriate for, say, a
contemporary restaurant brand that wants to "feel Heian". Most of the
time, reaching for the cultural tag as a marketing device is the
designer's ego, not the project's need. When in doubt: use the colors,
drop the costume.
