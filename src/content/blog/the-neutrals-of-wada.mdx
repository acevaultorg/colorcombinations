Most conversations about color are about the *colored* part. The red,
the blue, the accent. The hex value that matters.

But open almost any plate in Wada's dictionary and the thing that
actually holds the image together is the neutral. The un-colored color.
The paper. The chalk. The ink.

This post is about why those neutrals are the real structure of the
palette and a practical rule for picking the right one when you're
trying to build something on a screen.

## The neutrals Wada actually uses

English has more-or-less three words for a neutral: *white*, *grey*,
*black*. Japanese shikisai has dozens. Wada reaches for at least six
with regularity:

- <span lang="ja">生成</span> *kinari* (roughly `#F4EEE0`) — raw silk,
  unbleached. Warmest off-white in the tradition. Almost yellow.
- <span lang="ja">胡粉</span> *gofun* (roughly `#F8F4EA`) — chalk white
  made from ground shell, used by Edo-era painters to prime silk and
  paper. Cooler than kinari.
- <span lang="ja">鼠</span> *nezumi* (roughly `#949494`) — mouse grey.
  Not a cool slate; not a warm taupe. A specific, faintly green-brown
  grey that only makes sense once you've seen it.
- <span lang="ja">利休</span> *rikyū* (roughly `#8B8B78`) — Rikyū grey,
  named after the 16th-century tea master. Dusty sage. The signature
  neutral of the tea ceremony aesthetic.
- <span lang="ja">銀</span> *gin* (roughly `#CFD0C9`) — silver. Cooler
  than nezumi, lighter.
- <span lang="ja">墨</span> *sumi* (roughly `#111111`) — ink black. Not
  #000; warmer, with a trace of dark brown.

When a Wada plate reads "works", it is often because the third color —
the one designated as the background — is holding a specific tension
against the two foreground colors. *Kurenai* and *kon* (safflower
crimson and deep indigo) on `#F4EEE0` kinari is an old imperial combo.
The same crimson and navy on pure `#FFFFFF` looks like a flag. The
neutral is doing the work.

## Three rules of thumb

Three heuristics, pulled from staring at ~378 Wada plates for too long:

### Rule 1 — Warmer neutrals forgive more

`#F4EEE0` kinari against a saturated color tolerates a wider range of
secondary accents than `#FFFFFF` does. Pure white is merciless — every
accent has to earn its place. Kinari gives you half a step of slack on
saturation, a full step on temperature. Most editorial sites that
"feel right" use a warm off-white for exactly this reason.

### Rule 2 — The neutral should be on a plate you already like

Pick a plate whose foreground colors you'd use for a brand. Note the
third color. Use that. This is faster than color-theory reasoning and
it works because the plates are already balanced.

You can be explicit about this. On an editorial site, pick `kurenai-kon`
and the neutral is kinari `#F4EEE0`. On a sans-serif brand site, pick
`asagi-shu` (pale blue-green + vermillion) and the neutral is gofun
`#F8F4EA`. The plate does the picking for you.

### Rule 3 — Neutrals scale inversely to type size

The smaller your body text, the closer your neutral should be to pure
paper. `#F4EEE0` is lovely at display sizes and becomes a mud bath at
8px. Most of the hexes in Wada's neutral column were meant for paper,
ink, and silk — all of which render type at much higher contrast than
a backlit LCD does.

Practical corollary: set your display-type neutral warm, your body-
type neutral cool or near-white. The site in front of you right now
uses `#FAF7EF` (slightly warmed kinari) as the global background and
`#FFFFFF` as the elevated-surface color. Text contrast ratios were
tuned to WCAG AA against the warmer background, which is the harder
case.

## The common failure mode

The pattern I see over and over in portfolios, landing pages, and
client decks: a designer picks three saturated colors, drops them on
`#FFFFFF`, and wonders why the composition feels chilly or unreal.

The problem isn't the three colors. The problem is that `#FFFFFF` is
not a color the human visual system encounters naturally — it's only
ever backlit paper, screens, or freshly-bleached laundry. It carries
no cultural weight. It tastes like nothing.

The fix is almost always the same: shift the background 5–10 points
toward one of the four Japanese neutral-temperature anchors (warm-red
kinari, warm-green rikyū, neutral gin, cool grey nezumi). The
composition settles instantly because the background now has a
temperature and the foreground can play against it.
