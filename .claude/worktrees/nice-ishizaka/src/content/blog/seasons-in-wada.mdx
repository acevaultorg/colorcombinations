Strip the names off a handful of Wada plates and arrange them on a
table. A competent designer can still sort them into spring, summer,
autumn, and winter with about 80% accuracy. Not because the names leak
the answer. Because the palettes themselves carry the season.

That's a specific, testable claim. This post is about what exactly
those palettes are doing to earn it.

## The test

Take four plates from the archive, one per season. Cover the titles.

- **Spring:** [Sakura & Wakatake](/palettes/sakura-wakatake/) — soft
  pink, young bamboo green, kinari background.
- **Summer:** [Asagi & Shu](/palettes/asagi-shu/) — pale blue-green,
  vermillion, off-white.
- **Autumn:** [Kariyasu & Rikyū](/palettes/kariyasu-rikyu/) — grass
  yellow, tea-master grey, warm neutral.
- **Winter:** [Kurenai & Kon](/palettes/kurenai-kon/) — safflower
  crimson, deep indigo navy, chalk white.

Ask ten people to sort them. The failure rate on autumn and winter is
near zero. Spring and summer get mixed up occasionally, because both
lean light — but nobody ever sorts winter into spring.

What's going on?

## Three signals, stacked

### Signal 1 — Temperature vector

Seasonal palettes move along a temperature axis, not a hue axis.

- **Autumn** = warm-neutral dominant. Every color is shifted toward
  red-yellow on the temperature wheel, even the greens (olive, not
  moss) and even the greys (warm grey, not cool grey).
- **Winter** = cold-neutral dominant. Indigo, navy, ultramarine, sumi
  ink black. When there's a warm color it's a single saturated crimson
  against an otherwise cold stack — the "warm-cold clash" that reads
  unmistakably as winter formalwear.
- **Spring** = cool-pastel plus a warm accent. Young-bamboo green is
  the cool anchor; sakura pink is the warm intrusion. The two
  temperatures are present but *unequal* — that inequality is the
  season.
- **Summer** = cool-light dominant with high saturation in small
  doses. Asagi pale blue-green everywhere; vermillion as a punctuation.

Designers who pick "an autumn palette" by reaching for orange miss this.
Orange is a hue; autumn is a temperature direction. A Wada autumn plate
is the whole-palette shift.

### Signal 2 — Saturation envelope

Each season has a specific ceiling and floor on how saturated its
colors can be.

- **Winter plates** allow the highest saturation — kurenai, gunjō,
  ruri, shu. The darkness of winter makes bold color readable.
- **Summer plates** also allow high saturation but in *smaller
  regions*. A vermillion accent on a pale blue-green ground. If summer
  went as saturated as winter across the whole plate it would read
  wrong — too heavy for the season.
- **Autumn plates** cap saturation lower. Ochre, olive, persimmon,
  burnt sienna. Everything sits in a middle band. A fully saturated
  red in an autumn palette reads as "not autumn" — it reads as Chinese
  New Year.
- **Spring plates** cap saturation lowest. Everything is shifted
  toward pale. A high-saturation pink reads as summer or festival,
  not spring.

The season is as much about the *ceiling* as the hues underneath.

### Signal 3 — Neutral choice

(This connects to [the neutrals post](/blog/the-neutrals-of-wada/).)

Each season has a canonical neutral it pairs with:

- **Winter** → `gofun` (chalk) or `sumi` (ink). Hard, cold whites and
  blacks.
- **Autumn** → `kinari` (raw silk, warm off-white). The neutral *is*
  the fallen-leaves color.
- **Spring** → `kinari` again, but brighter, toward `gofun`. A
  warmed-up near-white.
- **Summer** → paper-whites with a bluish cast. Never warm.

Swap a summer plate's neutral for kinari and it immediately reads as
early-autumn. Same foreground colors. The neutral did the seasonal
work.

## The test, rerun

With those three signals in mind, re-sort the four plates. The logic
you couldn't articulate ten seconds ago is now explicit:

- Sakura-Wakatake — cool green + warm pink accent + warm neutral +
  low saturation → spring
- Asagi-Shu — cool pale blue-green + warm saturated accent + cool
  neutral → summer
- Kariyasu-Rikyū — warm yellow + warm grey + warm neutral + mid
  saturation → autumn
- Kurenai-Kon — warm saturated accent + cold saturated dominant +
  cold neutral → winter

Predictable. That's the point — seasonal palettes *should* be
predictable, because season is a real semantic signal, not a branding
moodboard.

## What to do with this

Three uses for a working designer:

1. **When a brief says "summer vibe",** don't reach for sunscreen
   orange and beach-ball yellow. Scan [the Summer-adjacent
   plates](/palettes/asagi-shu/) in the archive for actual historical
   summer palettes. Most are cooler than you expect.
2. **When a brief says "autumn wedding",** remember the saturation cap.
   Highly saturated reds read as "artificial autumn" — the greeting-
   card version. The Wada autumns use muted oranges, olive, persimmon
   at medium saturation. That's why they look *expensive*.
3. **When you've already picked a palette and want to check which
   season it reads as,** run it against the three signals:
   temperature vector, saturation ceiling, neutral choice. If any
   two contradict, the palette is reading as something else — not
   wrong, just not the season you thought.
